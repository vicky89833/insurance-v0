Whatthe deliverable

An enrollment model, built the way a production one would be

Given eight attributes about an employee — age, salary, tenure, employment type, region, gender, marital status, dependents — the system predicts whether they'll opt into a voluntary insurance product, and ranks them for outreach.

It ships as four things:

  • A training pipeline that validates, cleans, tunes six candidate models, picks one, and evaluates it on data it has never seen.
  • A prediction API — FastAPI, four endpoints, validated inputs, model loaded once at startup.
  • An evidence trail: every figure and metric regenerates from source, so no number in the write-up is unverifiable.
  • 76 tests covering data contracts, leakage prevention, threshold economics, and API failure modes.
Whythe finding

A perfect score is a red flag, not a trophy

The models hit 1.000 ROC-AUC on held-out data. That number should stop you cold. On real behavioural data it means a leaked feature; the only innocent explanation is that the label isn't really random.

So the pipeline checks. It fits an unconstrained decision tree on the whole dataset and asks how hard the data is to separate:

100% training accuracy at depth 5, using 13 leaves. No label noise, no irreducible error. A rule search then recovered the generator exactly — matching all 10,000 rows with zero mismatches.

Which reframes the whole exercise. Four of the eight features — gender, region, marital_status, tenure_years — are noise by construction. They appear nowhere in the rule, and permutation importance scores them at exactly zero. The perfect metric describes the data generator, not the modelling.

Why that's the valuable result

The tempting submission reports 100% accuracy and calls it a win. The honest one says: this dataset is synthetic and fully solved, here's the rule, and if it were real you wouldn't need machine learning to evaluate a three-of-four threshold — you'd need four lines of business logic. That check now runs on every pass of the analysis.

Howthe pipeline

Six stages, each defending against a specific failure

  1. 01
    Load & validateSchema checks and a data-quality report. Problems get reported, never silently patched — including 385 employees whose tenure exceeds their plausible working life.
  2. 02
    Preprocess inside the pipelineImputing, encoding and scaling are pipeline steps, so they're refit within every cross-validation fold. Fitting a scaler before the split is the classic silent leak; this makes it structurally impossible.
  3. 03
    Tune six candidatesFrom a majority-class dummy up to gradient boosting, 30 randomised-search candidates each, 5-fold stratified, scored on ROC-AUC.
  4. 04
    Select the simplest adequate modelA one-standard-error rule: among models statistically tied with the best, take the simplest. Complexity has to beat noise to earn its place.
  5. 05
    Evaluate once, on held-out dataRanking, calibration, per-subgroup slices, permutation importance, partial dependence — plus a cost-based decision threshold.
  6. 06
    ServeThe fitted pipeline persists to disk and loads once at API startup. Unknown categories encode safely rather than throwing.

The forest wins by 0.0003 AUC — on a 2,000-row test set, roughly half an employee's worth of ranking. Paying for that with 300 trees, an unreadable decision surface and 30× the training time is a bad trade. The tree is auditable by a compliance reviewer, scores in microseconds, and turns out to represent the true rule exactly.

The most informative row is the third one

Plain logistic regression manages 0.9691. The identical estimator over quantile-binned numerics reaches 0.9991 — closing 97% of the gap to the trees. That proves the baseline was limited by how the features were represented, not by the model's capacity: a straight line in the log-odds can't express a cliff at age 30, but a line over binned features can.

Howthe decision

A score isn't a decision

Turning "0.73" into "call this person" needs to know what each mistake costs. There's no real economics for this product, so the assumption is stated in the open rather than smuggled into a default: $25 of wasted outreach for a false positive, $300 of forgone margin for a missed enrollment.

Only the 12 : 1 ratio matters, and it moves the optimal cut-off from 0.5 down to 0.077. The business reading is more interesting than the number: when missing a customer costs twelve times more than a wasted call, you should contact nearly everyone with any meaningful probability.

Measured on an earlier, imperfect model: tuning the threshold cut operating cost from $19.62 to $3.34 per employee — while accuracy fell from 90.3% to 87.8%. Optimising accuracy would have cost roughly six times more to run.

Limitsread this

What doesn't transfer

  • Every number here is about synthetic data. No threshold, coefficient or score carries over to real enrollment behaviour.
  • The cost ratio is invented. $25 and $300 are placeholders that drive the recommended operating point. Replace them with measured campaign economics before trusting the threshold.
  • The fairness check passed trivially. Performance is identical across gender, region and employment type — but only because those fields are noise here. On real data this is the check most likely to fail, and gender would need legal review before any deployment.
  • The real model is missing its most important features. Enrollment is a price-sensitive decision, and this dataset contains no price: no premium, no employer contribution, no plan alternatives, no open-enrollment timing.

The judgement call worth arguing about

Because the top models tie on every fold, their standard error is exactly zero — which would make the selection rule demand a perfect tie and always crown the most complex model. The tolerance is floored at 0.0005 AUC to keep the "prefer simpler" intent alive. That constant is a judgement call, and it's named in the config rather than buried.

Reproducing all of it

make eda     # quality report, rule discovery, figures
make train   # tune, select, evaluate, persist
